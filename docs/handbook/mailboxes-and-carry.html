Understand · Explanation

Mailboxes and carry

What happens when the person you write to is not reachable right now.

TL;DR

What: two mechanisms. A post office (a host with a disk) keeps a message for someone who is offline and hands it over when they come back. Carry lets handheld radios take a message with them when no route exists and give it to the recipient when they meet.

Why: radios are switched off, batteries die and people walk out of range. A network that only delivers when both sides are online is not much use outdoors.

Advantages

  • Nothing to log in to and no sync interval to wait for
  • The sender sees delivered from mailbox
  • Three copies on different hosts, so one failing host does not lose mail
  • Carry works with no repeaters and no internet

Good to know

  • Mail between nodes that have written to each other this week is sealed to a weekly key that is erased about three weeks later (since 0.9.13); other mail is sealed without forward secrecy
  • A post office or carrier can delete or withhold what it holds
  • Mail is kept at most 7 days, 100 messages per recipient
  • Carry costs a little extra airtime and helps only when people actually meet

working copy Post offices and the LoRa trunks were built and flashed on a desk; carry is simulated. No field measurement exists.

Your friend's radio is off

The life of a message to someone who is offline

Press Play. The times are the real constants of the post office protocol.

Constants: DEPOSIT_AFTER_MS 45 s, three replica hosts, MailSync every 6 hours and when a post office names you, presence checks at most every 10 minutes (spec 8.1).

  1. You send a message. If no confirmation arrives within 45 seconds, your node hands the message to its own home post office.
  2. The post office keeps it and sends copies to the recipient's three replica hosts over the internet backbone.
  3. When the recipient's radio comes back and announces itself, a post office that hears it delivers the mail. If the recipient is somewhere else, it asks for its mail, and the post office delivers over whatever route reaches it now.
  4. The recipient's node sends a small receipt back to you, and your app shows delivered.
Who keeps mail today

Hosts (a PC or a server with a disk) keep mail. Repeaters on radio boards carry mail but do not keep it, because a small flash holds only 30 to 40 messages for everyone in range.

When there are no repeaters: relay and carry

What carry gives in a crisis

The national network is hit: no internet, the four biggest towns destroyed, a third of the remaining repeaters gone, thirty handhelds driving between towns. Share delivered, by distance.

Carry helps most at middle distances, 66 to 76 % at 25 to 100 km. Over 100 km it moves 9 to 14 %. It costs about 24 % more airtime. Keeping messages longer, with more room, did no better.

Source: tools/sim-experiments/crisis.mjs, 8 simulated hours, mean of 3 seeds, run 2026-11-27. simulated

  • Relay role: a handheld that hears no repeater switches to relay. It forwards for up to 4 hops, and waits a little first so a real repeater would win.
  • Carry: a message that no route can deliver is kept by the sender and handed over to people it meets, who hand it on until it reaches the recipient.
  • Delivered notice: when the recipient gets a carried copy, it sends a tiny notice, and carriers delete their copies.

What post offices give a traveller

A traveller from Aalborg to Rønne switches the phone off for 50 minutes. Share of the messages written while the phone was off that arrived.

With the internet up, post offices take it from 4 % to 76 %, and 88 % when repeaters also act as small offices. With the internet down, post offices still deliver 39 %. They are not free: in this run about 2.5 times the airtime, and delivery once the traveller was on Bornholm fell from 99 % to 54 %, which the run does not explain.

Post office protocol

Source: maarse-core/src/mail.rs, maarse-host/src/node.rs, spec section 8.1. working copy

Who is a post office

Post offices say so in their hellos and announces with a 2-bit class: 0 none, 1 small flash, 2 megabytes, 3 a host with a disk. Hosts are class 3; radio nodes are class 0 today. Every node picks the best post office among its neighbours (highest class, then best SNR) as its home post office and names it in its announce.

Dropping mail

When a direct message has no end-to-end acknowledgement within DEPOSIT_AFTER_MS = 45 s, the sender sends a Deposit to its home post office. The message is sealed without a session, because a ratchet message delivered days later may fall outside the skipped-key window: to the recipient's weekly mail key if we have one that is at most a week old (since 0.9.13; the key is erased after about three weeks), otherwise in the static format, without forward secrecy.

Deposit payload = recipient (16) | op (1) | body
opNameBodyMeaning
0STOREsealed messageKeep it for the recipient
1SYNCIDs already heldDeliver the recipient's mail to it, wherever it is
2FETCHasker, IDs heldSend the rest to the asker as STORE
3DELIVEREDIDsDelete them
4BUNDLEn × (length, sealed message)Messages sent to the recipient, packed into as few packets as fit
5RECEIPTreceipt IDsRecipient to the original sender: the mail arrived

Delivery receipts

The post office does not know who sent a message, and the recipient's acknowledgement goes to the post office. So after opening mail that may have come from a post office (a bundle, mail sealed to its weekly or account key, or a message too long to bundle), the recipient, who now knows the sender from the decrypted message, sends the sender a RECEIPT. The sender recomputes the receipt ID for the copies in its outbox and marks the match as delivered.

Copies

A host that takes a message keeps it and sends copies to the recipient's three replica hosts: the three highest rendezvous scores over the hosts in its DHT table. Every host computes the same three for a given recipient, so when a host joins or leaves, only the recipients it ranked first move. Copies travel over the DHT's TCP requests and never over radio.

Asking for mail

A node sends a MailSync (two-hop flood) when it starts, when its home post office changes, when a post office's hello names it, and every 6 hours. The post office's hello carries up to four path IDs of recipients with mail waiting, like the traffic indication in a Wi-Fi beacon. A host that hears a node's announce over its own radio asks the node's replicas for mail, at most every 10 minutes per node, the way a mobile network delivers SMS when a phone registers.

Carry protocol

Source: spec section 5.10. simulated Firmware relays are set to 4 copies; carry is off elsewhere.

Carry is enabled when carry_copies > 0. A message the router gives up on (the path request failed, or no acknowledgement came after all retries) is carried using binary spray and wait: the carrier holds carry_copies tokens.

ConstantValue
CARRY_TTL_MS30 minutes
CARRY_MAX8 messages
CARRY_EVERY_MS5 s between looks at the neighbours
CARRY_FRESH_MS30 s: neighbours heard within this window count
CARRY_RETRY_MS60 s between repeated handoffs to the destination
CARRY_REROUTE_MS300 s: a sender near a repeater retries ordinary routing

At each step the carrier looks at its fresh neighbours. If the destination is among them, the message is handed over. Otherwise, if it holds more than one token, it gives half of them to a newly met non-repeater neighbour that has not had a copy. A handoff carries tokens (1) | time left in s (2) | from (2) | message. The destination delivers it as an ordinary Data message, still end-to-end encrypted.

The delivered notice is a type 11 packet with no path and an empty payload, 18 bytes on air. Its destination is ack_token of the message, so it names nobody. A carrier that hears it deletes its copy and remembers the notice for the copy's lifetime (at most 16 notices), passes it on once if it gave copies away, and repeats it when it meets a neighbour it exchanged a copy with. When it reaches the original sender, the router reports CarryAcked.

Notices are not entered in the duplicate filter, so they can be repeated.
